How many logo concepts should you actually get?

One designer quotes ₹8,000 for three concepts. Another quotes ₹6,000 for twelve. The second looks like better value and is almost always worse, for a reason that becomes obvious once you divide the money by the options.

A logo concept is not a drawing. It is a decision about what the mark should say, followed by the drawing that tests it. The decision is most of the work — reading the market, looking at what competitors already own, choosing which of several possible directions the business should stand in.

Twelve concepts at ₹6,000 gives each one ₹500 of attention. Nobody researches a market, considers a direction and tests a mark for ₹500. What you get instead is one idea drawn twelve ways, or twelve template shapes with your name set beneath them.

The number of options is not a measure of effort. It is usually a measure of how thinly the effort has been spread.

Why many options make deciding harder

There is a second cost, and clients feel it before they can name it. Presented with twelve marks, you stop judging whether any of them is right for the business and start judging which one you like the look of. Those are different questions, and the second one has no correct answer.

Twelve options turns your logo into a matter of taste. Three well-argued ones keep it a business decision.

It also spreads the decision. Twelve marks get forwarded to family, staff and the WhatsApp group, everyone picks a favourite, and you end up choosing the least objectionable rather than the strongest. I have watched good marks lose to weak ones this way more than once.

What three concepts should look like

Genuinely different directions, not variations. If two of the three could be described in the same sentence, you have two concepts and a spare.
