Why your logo looks wrong on the signboard

The file was approved, the fabricator was paid, and the board went up looking thicker, muddier and somehow cheaper than the version on your phone. Here is what usually went wrong, and the four checks that prevent it.

Every few months someone sends me a photo of a new shop board with the message "this doesn't look like the design". Usually they are right, and usually nobody made a mistake. The logo was drawn for a screen and then asked to survive a completely different medium.

Screen light, vinyl doesn't

A phone emits light. A signboard reflects it, and at night it reflects whatever the street lamp gives it. Contrast that felt bold on a laptop turns flat outdoors, especially in the mid-tones. Dark grey on black is a screen decision; on a board at dusk it reads as a black rectangle.

This is why I test every logo as a flat two-colour version before anything else. If the mark holds up in pure black on white, it will hold up in vinyl, embroidery and a newspaper ad. If it needs a gradient to look good, it was never a logo — it was an illustration.

If the mark needs a gradient to look good, it was never a logo.

Thin strokes disappear

Vinyl cutters have a physical limit. Anything under roughly a millimetre at final size either tears when the weeder pulls the sheet or gets quietly thickened by the operator so it survives. That fine hairline under your wordmark, the one that looked elegant at 200% zoom, is the first casualty.

Same story with letter spacing. Two characters sitting close together on screen will bridge into one blob once the vinyl is applied and the edges settle. Rounded serifs and tight counters — the enclosed spaces inside letters like a, e and g — fill in first.

Rule of thumb

Print the logo at the smallest size it will ever appear, tape it to a wall, and walk back three metres. Whatever you cannot read at that distance will not exist on the board either.

The colour shift nobody warned you about

Your screen shows RGB. Vinyl comes in fixed catalogue shades, flex printing works in CMYK, and neither can reproduce the bright saturated blues and greens a display can. A vivid teal will come back duller and slightly greyer, every time, from every vendor in the city.

The fix is to choose the physical colour first. Ask the fabricator for their vinyl swatch book, pick the actual shade in daylight, and let the digital version match that — not the other way round. For printed flex, a Pantone reference on the artwork gives the press something objective to hit.

Four checks before cutting

  1. 01 Send vector, not PNG. A JPEG or PNG scaled to board size will be traced by someone else, and their trace is not your logo.
  2. 02 Convert text to outlines. Otherwise the fabricator's machine substitutes a font it happens to have, and nobody notices until it is on the wall.
  3. 03 Approve a single-colour version. Ask specifically to see the logo in solid black. Any weakness shows up there first.
  4. 04 See a sample cut. A one-foot test piece costs very little compared with redoing a ten-foot board.

None of this requires you to become a production expert. It requires whoever designed the logo to have thought about the signboard before drawing it — which is the whole argument for designing the mark and its applications in the same place.
