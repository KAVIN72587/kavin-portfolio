An interior design portfolio that wins projects

Interior designers and architects are judged on their work, and most of their websites are a long gallery of beautiful photos with no context. Visitors scroll, admire, and leave without knowing what the project cost, how long it took or whether you would take on a job like theirs.

Here is how to structure an interior design or architecture website so it attracts the projects you want, answers the questions clients have before calling, and filters out enquiries that are not a fit.

01

Project pages, not one big gallery

Give each project its own page. A short story: the client's brief, the constraints (a narrow flat, a tight budget, a rented space), what you designed and the result. Ten to fifteen well-chosen photos per project, in a logical order, room by room. Add the location area, size and type, such as 2BHK apartment, clinic or café.

A prospective client looking at a similar project sees themselves in it. That is far more persuasive than a hundred unconnected photos.

02

Photography is the whole portfolio

Your website is only as good as the photos. Professional photography of finished projects, in daylight, styled and uncluttered, is the best investment you can make in your marketing. Phone photos taken at handover with workers' tools still around undersell the work.

Before-and-after shots, from the same angle, are especially effective for renovation work.

Ask clients' permission to photograph and publish their homes, and do not show addresses or identifying details.

03

Explain your process

Clients worry about the unknown: how long, how many visits, when do they pay, who manages the contractors. A short process page, such as consultation, concept, design and drawings, approval, execution, handover, with rough timelines, removes that uncertainty. Say whether you do design only or design and execution, because clients often do not know the difference.

Mention how you charge too: a percentage of project cost, a fixed design fee, or per square foot. Clients compare designers on very different pricing models without realising it, and a short explanation builds trust before the first meeting.

04

Give budget guidance

The question every client has and few websites answer: what will this cost? You do not have to publish a price list, but ranges help, such as "modular kitchens typically from ₹… depending on size and materials" or "full 2BHK interiors from ₹…". It filters out enquiries that are far from your range and saves both sides a site visit.

05

An enquiry form that qualifies

Ask the questions you would ask on the first call: type of property, location, size, scope (one room, full home, office), timeline and rough budget. A few well-chosen fields are better than a long form. You get better leads, and the client feels taken seriously because you asked about their project specifically.

06

Make it fast despite the photos

Image-heavy websites are often painfully slow on phones, which is where most people first see them. Photos should be resized and compressed for the web, loaded as the visitor scrolls, and served in modern formats. A portfolio that takes ten seconds to load loses people before they see your best work.

The fix is technical but not complicated: export photos at the size they are displayed, typically no wider than about 2,000 pixels, compress them, and let the site load images only as they come into view. Done properly, a project page with fifteen large photos can still open quickly on mobile data, and the photos look just as sharp on screen.

Show the work you want more of

Your portfolio attracts more of what it shows. If you want more commercial projects, lead with commercial projects, even if you have done more homes. If you want premium work, show fewer, better projects rather than every job you have ever done.

Curate for the client you want, not the clients you have had.

Six excellent, well-documented projects beat thirty average ones. Remove old work that no longer represents your style.

Pages for a design studio site

  1. Home. Your style in one line, three best projects, enquiry button.
  2. Projects. One page per project with story, details and photos.
  3. Services and process. Design only or design and build, steps and timelines.
  4. About. Your background, team, approach.
  5. Enquiry. Qualifying form, phone, WhatsApp, studio address.

Add new projects as they are photographed, and retire weaker ones. A portfolio that changes every few months also gives you something to share on Instagram and LinkedIn.

Quick answers

What should an interior designer's website include?

Individual project pages with a short story and professional photos, a clear process, services offered, budget guidance, an about page and a qualifying enquiry form.

Should interior designers show prices on their website?

A full price list is not necessary, but typical budget ranges for common projects help clients self-qualify and reduce wasted site visits.

Why is my portfolio website slow?

Usually because photos are uploaded at full camera size. Resizing and compressing images, using modern formats and loading them as the visitor scrolls fixes most cases.
