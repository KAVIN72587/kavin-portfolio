One recording, ten clips

You recorded an hour-long conversation. It went up on YouTube, got a few hundred views, and that was that. Inside it, there were probably ten moments that could each have reached more people than the whole episode did.

Repurposing — cutting short, vertical clips from long recordings — is one of the most efficient things a business or creator can do with video. The hard work of getting people in a room, setting up, and talking is already done. What is left is finding the moments and shaping them for phones.

Here is how that edit works, and how to record with it in mind.

01

Finding the clips is the real work

A good short is a complete thought in under a minute: a strong opinion, a surprising fact, a short story, a clear tip, a funny moment. It has to make sense to someone who never saw the episode. Finding these means watching or reading through the whole recording and marking moments that stand alone.

AI tools that pick clips automatically are improving, and they are useful for a first pass. They still miss context, cut mid-sentence and pick moments that only make sense if you heard the previous five minutes. A human pass is what turns their suggestions into clips worth posting.

Ask your guests and hosts to answer questions in full sentences. "Yes, exactly" cannot become a clip.

02

The first two seconds decide everything

On Reels and Shorts, people decide almost immediately whether to keep watching. Each clip should open on its strongest line — sometimes moved from the middle of the answer to the start — with a short on-screen headline that tells viewers what they are about to get. The long version can take its time. The short version cannot.

03

Reframing for vertical

A podcast is usually filmed wide, horizontally, with two or three people. Shorts are vertical. Each clip has to be reframed: cropping in on whoever is talking, switching between speakers, or stacking two people one above the other. This is why recording in 4K helps — there is room to crop without losing quality.

04

Captions are not optional

Most short-form video is watched on mute, at least at first. Large, readable captions, timed word by word, keep people watching long enough to turn the sound on. For Tamil, English or mixed conversations, captions need proofreading; automatic tools still make mistakes with names, technical terms and code-switching.

05

Volume makes it cheaper per clip

Once the recording is reviewed and the style is set, each additional clip is faster to produce. That is why repurposing works best as a batch: ten clips from one episode cost far less per clip than one clip ordered on its own. For reference, my reels start at ₹1,500 each, and monthly packs bring the per-reel price down.

Record for the clips, not just the episode

If you know you will cut shorts, a few changes on recording day make them far better. Use separate microphones for each person. Frame each speaker so they can be cropped to vertical. Ask questions that invite a story or a strong opinion. And leave a beat of silence after each answer so the editor has a clean place to cut.

Plan the shorts before you press record, and the edit becomes selection rather than rescue.

Keep a running list during the conversation of moments that felt strong. Five timestamps noted live are worth an hour of searching later.

A simple monthly workflow

  1. Week 1: record one long episode. A conversation, interview, workshop or talk, ideally forty minutes or more.
  2. Week 1: publish the full version. On YouTube or as a podcast, with chapters.
  3. Week 2: get eight to twelve clips cut. Each with a hook, captions and a vertical frame.
  4. Weeks 2–4: post two or three clips a week. On Reels, Shorts and LinkedIn, each pointing back to the full episode.
  5. Week 4: check what worked. Which topics got watched to the end? Use that to plan the next episode's questions.

What makes a clip worth cutting

When reviewing a recording, I mark moments that pass at least two of these tests:

  1. It makes sense with no context. Someone scrolling past who has never heard of you understands it.
  2. It has a clear point. A tip, an opinion, a number, a mistake to avoid.
  3. It has energy. The speaker leans in, laughs, disagrees, or tells a story.
  4. It ends cleanly. The thought finishes, rather than trailing into the next question.

Clips that pass all four are the ones worth putting money behind as ads, too.

Quick answers

How many shorts can I get from a one-hour podcast?

Usually eight to fifteen strong clips, depending on how much of the conversation is self-contained. Interviews with clear questions and full answers produce more usable clips than loose chats.

Can I use the same clip on Reels, Shorts and LinkedIn?

Yes, usually with small changes. The vertical 9:16 format works on all three. Remove other platforms' watermarks, and adjust the caption and first line of text for each audience.

Are AI clipping tools good enough?

They are good at suggesting candidate moments and generating rough captions quickly. They are weaker at judging context, trimming cleanly and choosing the hook. Using one for the first pass and a human editor to finish is a sensible mix.
