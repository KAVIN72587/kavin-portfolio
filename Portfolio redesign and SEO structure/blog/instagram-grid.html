Your Instagram grid is not a portfolio

Businesses spend real effort making nine squares look coordinated. It is time spent on the one view most of your audience will never open, while the view they do see gets no attention at all.

Someone visits your profile page in one of two situations: they are deciding whether to follow you, or they already know you and are looking for something specific. Both matter. Neither involves admiring how the tenth post relates to the seventh.

Everything else — the discovery, the sharing, the actual reason anyone finds you — happens in the feed and on the Reels tab, where your post appears alone between two unrelated ones.

Where the two views disagree

Decision
Good for the grid
Good in the feed

Colour

Muted, similar tones so the squares sit together calmly.

Enough contrast to interrupt a scroll. Calm reads as skippable.

Text on image

Little or none — text clutters a nine-square view.

A short line of large text, because the caption is collapsed and unread.

Cropping

Consistent framing across every post.

Whatever crop makes this one image legible on a 6-inch screen.

Post order

Arranged so neighbours complement each other.

Irrelevant. Each post appears alone between two strangers' posts.

When the two disagree, the feed wins. That is where people decide whether to stop.

What to design for instead

One idea per post

It is being seen alone, at thumbnail size, for about a second. A post carrying three messages delivers none of them.

Legible at a glance

Hold your phone at arm's length. If you cannot read the main line, it is too small — regardless of how it looks on your laptop.

Vertical, not square

Vertical occupies more of the screen as it passes. Square is a habit from an older version of the app.

Consistent style, not a consistent grid

Same typeface, same two or three colours, same framing logic. Recognisable in isolation, which is where recognition matters.

Say the useful thing

The price, the location, the offer, the opening time. Businesses withhold these hoping for a DM, and mostly get a scroll instead.

What the profile page is actually for

Not aesthetics — answers. Someone has landed there because a reel caught them, and they have about four seconds of curiosity. In that time they want to know what you sell, whether you are near them, and how to reach you.

Which means the bio line, the location, the highlight covers and the pinned posts are doing more work than the grid arrangement ever will. Three pinned posts that answer what you sell, what it costs and where you are will convert better than any colour scheme.

A coordinated grid is a nice thing to have. A clear bio and three useful pinned posts are the thing that gets you the enquiry.

None of this argues for inconsistency. A recognisable visual style genuinely helps, because a returning viewer identifies your post before reading the name. But that consistency should live in your type, your colour and your framing — things visible in a single post — rather than in how nine of them arrange themselves.
