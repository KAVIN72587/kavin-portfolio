The design agreement that prevents arguments

Most disagreements between businesses and designers are not about quality. They are about assumptions: the client thought the visiting card was included, the designer thought two revisions meant two, the client expected the editable file, the designer expected the balance before delivery. A short written agreement settles all of these before they become problems.

You do not need a long legal document for most small design projects. A clear quote or agreement covering a few key points is enough. Here is what it should include.

01

Scope and deliverables

List exactly what will be designed. Not "branding", but "logo with three initial concepts, visiting card front and back, letterhead template". Include sizes, formats and quantities where they matter, such as "12 social media posts and 4 story templates per month".

Equally useful is a line about what is not included, such as printing, copywriting, photography or website changes. This avoids surprises later.

02

Timeline

Write the key dates: when the first concepts will be shared, how long each revision takes and when final files will be delivered. Include your responsibilities too, such as sending content, photos and feedback by certain dates. Delays on either side should move the schedule fairly.

03

Revisions

State how many revision rounds are included and what counts as a round. Mention what happens if more are needed, usually an hourly or per-round fee agreed before starting. Clarify that a completely new direction after approval is a new concept, not a revision.

04

Payment terms

Write the total price, the advance amount, when the balance is due and how payment will be made. For larger projects, list the stages. Mention whether GST applies and whether there are extra costs like stock images, fonts or printing.

A common and fair structure is an advance to start and the balance on delivery of final files.

Link the final payment to the delivery of final files. It keeps both sides motivated to complete the project properly.

05

Files and handover

List the files you will receive at the end: vector formats, PNGs, print-ready PDFs, editable templates and fonts or font links. If the editable working file is extra, say so. This single section prevents more disputes than any other.

06

Ownership and rights

State that full rights to use the final design pass to you once the project is paid in full. Many designers keep the right to show the work in their portfolio, which is normal, and you can ask to delay this if the project is confidential.

If the design includes stock images, fonts or illustrations with their own licences, the agreement should mention it.

07

If plans change

Cover cancellation: what happens to the advance if you stop the project, and what you receive for work already done. Cover pauses too: if a project is on hold for months, can it be restarted at the same price? A sentence or two on each is enough.

Clear terms protect both sides

A design agreement is not about distrust. It is a shared understanding written down, so nobody has to rely on memory or WhatsApp scrolling months later. Professional designers are happy to provide one, and clients who ask for one tend to have smoother projects.

If it isn't written down, it isn't agreed.

For small projects, a detailed quote accepted by message can serve as the agreement. For larger or long-term work, a signed document is worth the extra effort.

Design agreement checklist

  1. Scope. What is included and what is not.
  2. Timeline. Dates for concepts, revisions, finals and your inputs.
  3. Revisions. Number of rounds and cost of extras.
  4. Payment. Total, advance, stages, GST and method.
  5. Files. Exact formats delivered at the end.
  6. Rights. Ownership after full payment, portfolio use.
  7. Changes. Cancellation and pause terms.

This is practical guidance, not legal advice. For high-value projects or trademark matters, consult a lawyer.

Keep the final agreement somewhere easy to find, along with the invoices and the final files. If a question comes up a year later, about who owns a design or what was included, everything you need is in one place. Most businesses never need to look at it again, which is exactly the point. Good paperwork is the kind you forget you have.

If a designer sends you a quote without these details, it is perfectly reasonable to ask them to add them. You are not being difficult; you are making sure both of you remember the same thing.

Quick answers

Do I need a contract with a freelance designer?

For small projects, a detailed written quote accepted by both sides is usually enough. For larger or ongoing work, a signed agreement is recommended.

What should a design contract include?

Scope and deliverables, timeline, revision rounds, payment terms, files to be delivered, ownership of the design and what happens if the project is cancelled.

Who owns the design after the project?

Usually the client, once the project is paid in full, but this should be stated in writing. Designers commonly keep the right to show the work in their portfolio.
