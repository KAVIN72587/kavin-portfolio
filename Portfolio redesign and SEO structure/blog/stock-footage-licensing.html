Stock footage: useful, risky, often generic

A smiling team high-fiving in a glass office. A hand pointing at a graph. A drone shot of a city that is clearly not Chennai. Stock footage can fill gaps in a video quickly, and it can also make a business look like every other business. It comes with licence terms that matter too.

Here is when stock footage genuinely helps, how licences work in plain terms, the difference between free and paid libraries, and how to use stock without making your video feel generic. Licence terms vary by library and change over time, so always read the current terms.

01

When stock footage helps

Stock is useful for things you cannot film easily: aerial city views, nature, abstract backgrounds, generic concepts like "technology" or "growth", locations far away, or shots that would need expensive permissions. It also fills small gaps when a shoot missed something, such as a transition or an establishing shot.

02

When it hurts

Stock footage of people in place of your actual team, customers or premises undermines trust. Viewers notice when the "customers" are obviously models in a different country. For anything that represents who you are, film the real thing, even on a phone. Use stock for context, never as a substitute for your own business.

It can also be misleading. Showing a gleaming factory floor or a large team that is not yours implies something untrue about your business. Customers who later visit, or meet the real team, notice the gap.

03

How stock licences work

Buying or downloading a stock clip gives you a licence to use it under certain conditions, not ownership. Standard licences usually allow use in marketing videos, social media and websites, often with limits on audience size, broadcast use or use in products for resale. Extended licences cover more. Some free libraries allow broad commercial use without attribution; others require credit or exclude certain uses.

Keep a record of each clip's source and licence, ideally a screenshot or the licence certificate, with the project.

04

Editorial-only clips

Some stock footage is marked "editorial use only". These clips, often showing recognisable people, brands, events or places, can be used for news and commentary but not in advertising or promotional content. Using them in a business advert can lead to claims from the people or brands shown. Check every clip for this label.

05

Recognisable people and property

Commercial stock with recognisable people should come with model releases, which the stock library confirms. Footage of private property or artwork may need property releases. Reputable paid libraries handle this; for free clips, check carefully, because some uploads lack proper releases.

06

Free vs paid libraries

Free libraries such as Pexels and Pixabay offer large collections under broad licences, and quality has improved a lot. The downside is that popular free clips appear in thousands of videos. Paid libraries offer more choice, more specific and higher-quality clips, and clearer legal protection. For ads and important brand videos, paid stock is often worth it.

Whichever you use, download from the library itself, not from a site reposting its clips.

07

Making stock feel less generic

Choose clips that match your real setting: Indian streets, Indian offices and Indian customers, where relevant. Grade stock clips to match your own footage, so they do not jump out. Use them briefly, as part of a sequence, rather than as long featured shots. Combine them with text and your own footage so they support your message instead of carrying it.

Your own footage is your strongest asset

Stock footage can make a video look polished, but it cannot make it look like you. The most persuasive business videos show the real team, real place and real product, with stock used sparingly for context.

Use stock for the world around you, never for who you are.

If a video relies heavily on stock, consider whether a simple shoot of your own premises, or an animated explainer, would communicate better.

Before using a stock clip

  1. Licence. Does it allow commercial and, if needed, paid-ad use?
  2. Editorial label. Never use editorial-only clips in promotions.
  3. Releases. Recognisable people and property covered?
  4. Fit. Does it look like your market and setting?
  5. Record. Source and licence saved with the project.

A minute spent checking these for each clip protects you from claims later and keeps the video consistent with your brand.

Quick answers

Can I use free stock footage in a business video?

Usually yes, if the library's licence allows commercial use. Check the terms for each clip, including any attribution requirements and restrictions.

What does editorial use only mean?

It means the clip can be used for news or commentary, not in advertising or promotional content. Using it in a business ad can lead to claims.

Do I own stock footage after buying it?

No. You receive a licence to use it under certain conditions. The rights stay with the creator or library.
