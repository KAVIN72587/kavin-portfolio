What patients look for on a clinic website

Someone searching for a dentist, a physiotherapist or a paediatrician is usually worried, in pain, or short on time. They are not browsing. They want to know three things fast: can this clinic help me, when can I go, and how do I get there?

Most clinic websites answer none of these on the first screen. They open with a stock photo of a smiling doctor and a paragraph about "excellence in healthcare". Here is what patients actually look for, in roughly the order they look for it.

This is about design and information, not medical content. What a clinic may say about treatments and results is governed by professional rules, and that is between the doctor and their council — I keep clinic sites factual for that reason.

01

Timings, location and phone, on the first screen

Opening hours for each day, including Sunday and lunch breaks. The area name in plain words — "Velachery, near the MRTS station" — plus an embedded map. A phone number you can tap to call, and a WhatsApp button. If patients have to scroll or click to find these, many will call the next clinic instead.

Make sure the hours match your Google Business Profile exactly. Wrong hours are the most common complaint in clinic reviews.

02

Who the doctors are

Patients choose a doctor, not a building. Each doctor should have a real photo, their qualifications as registered, their specialisation, languages spoken, and the days they consult. A short, plain-language line on the kind of patients they usually see helps far more than a long list of conferences.

03

What the clinic treats, in patients' words

List conditions and services the way patients describe them: "back pain", "braces for children", "knee pain after sports", not only the clinical terms. Each major service can have its own short page explaining what to expect at a visit, how long it takes, and whether a referral is needed. Keep it factual: no promises of outcomes, no before-and-after claims.

04

An easy way to book

Some clinics need a full online booking system. Many do not. A short form (name, phone, preferred day, reason) or a WhatsApp message that arrives pre-filled with "I'd like an appointment" is often enough, and much simpler for staff to manage. What matters is that the booking route is obvious and that someone responds the same day.

05

Practical details that remove doubts

Consultation fee or fee range, if you are comfortable sharing it. Parking. Wheelchair access. Insurance or cashless arrangements, if any. What to bring to a first visit. These are the questions receptionists answer on the phone all day; putting them on the website saves calls and brings in patients who are ready.

Trust comes from facts, not adjectives

"Best clinic in Chennai" and "world-class care" make patients more sceptical, not less. What builds trust is checkable detail: the doctor's registration, years of practice, real photos of the clinic, clear prices, and an honest description of what a visit involves.

Show the clinic as it is. Patients are reassured by what they recognise when they arrive.

Link to your Google reviews rather than copying them onto the site. Patients trust reviews more when they can see them in the original place.

Patient privacy on the website

A clinic website collects sensitive information the moment it has a booking form. A few basics:

  1. Ask only for what you need to book. Name, phone, preferred time. Medical details can wait for the consultation.
  2. Use HTTPS. The padlock in the browser. Any decent host provides it free.
  3. Have a short privacy page. What you collect, why, and who sees it. Plain language is fine.
  4. Do not post patient photos or stories without written consent. Even with consent, think twice about anything identifiable.

Five things to check on your own site today

Open your clinic website on your phone, on mobile data, and time yourself:

  1. Can you find today's timings in under five seconds? If not, move them to the top.
  2. Does tapping the phone number start a call? A number shown as an image cannot be tapped.
  3. Does the map open directions to the right entrance? Especially in a building with several clinics.
  4. Is every doctor listed still consulting there? Out-of-date doctor lists cause awkward phone calls.
  5. Does a booking request actually reach someone? Send a test. Forms that quietly fail are more common than you would think.

Quick answers

Does a small clinic need a website?

If patients choose you by searching or by comparing clinics nearby, yes. A clear site with timings, doctors, services and an easy booking route turns searches into appointments, and supports your Google Business Profile.

Should a clinic website show consultation fees?

It helps if you can. Many patients hesitate to call because they do not know what it will cost. A fee or fee range removes that barrier. If fees vary, explain what they depend on.

Do I need online appointment booking?

Not always. A short enquiry form or a pre-filled WhatsApp message is enough for many clinics. Full booking systems make sense for busy multi-doctor practices where slots fill up and staff time is stretched.
