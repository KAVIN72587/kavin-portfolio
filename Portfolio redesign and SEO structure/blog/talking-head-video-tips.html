Talking to camera without looking stiff

Most business owners are excellent at explaining their work across a table. Put a phone in front of them and they freeze, speed up, read in a monotone or forget their point. It is not a talent problem. It is a setup and habit problem, and both are fixable.

Here is how to set up a simple talking-head shoot, how to look and sound natural, and how to record in a way that makes editing easy and the final video feel relaxed.

01

The setup: steady, eye level, close

Put the phone on a tripod or a stack of books, at eye level. Below eye level looks up your nose; above looks down on you. Frame from roughly mid-chest to just above the head, with your eyes about a third of the way down the frame. Clean the lens. Use the rear camera if you can, as it is usually better, with a mirror or a second phone to check framing.

02

Light from the front

Face a window during the day. Soft daylight on your face is flattering and free. Avoid sitting with the window behind you, which turns you into a silhouette, and avoid harsh overhead tube lights, which create shadows under the eyes. If you film at night, a simple ring light or a lamp with a white shade in front of you works.

03

Sound: a mic close to you

Clip a lapel microphone to your shirt, a hand's width below the chin. It makes a bigger difference than any camera upgrade. Turn off fans and the AC while recording if you can, and choose a room with soft furnishings to reduce echo.

Record ten seconds and play it back on headphones before the real take.

04

Where to look

Look into the lens, not at your own image on screen. It feels strange at first, but it is what makes viewers feel you are speaking to them. Put a small sticker beside the lens as a reminder. If you are being interviewed instead, look at the interviewer, slightly to one side of the camera.

05

Energy: a little more than normal

Cameras flatten energy. What feels like slightly too much enthusiasm in the room looks normal on screen. Smile at the start, speak a fraction slower than usual, and let your hands move naturally just inside the frame. Imagine you are explaining to one specific customer you like, not to "an audience".

Stand up if you can. Many people speak with more energy standing than sitting.

06

Talk in short chunks

Do not try to deliver two minutes in one perfect take. Speak one point, or two or three sentences, then pause. If you stumble, stop, take a breath, and start that sentence again from the beginning. The editor will cut out the mistakes and join the good takes. Short chunks also let the editor cut to B-roll and keep the pace up.

07

What to wear

Solid, medium colours work best. Avoid fine stripes and small checks, which shimmer on camera, and avoid pure white or black, which lose detail. Wear what you would wear to meet a client, so the video matches who you are when they meet you. A lapel mic needs a collar or a button placket to clip to.

The first five minutes are always the worst

Almost everyone is stiff for the first few minutes on camera and then relaxes. Plan for it: start recording, talk about something easy for a few minutes, and only then begin the content you want to use. Many of the best takes come after twenty minutes, not two.

Record more than you need, relax, and let the edit find the best version of you.

Batch your recording. Once you are warmed up, record four or five short videos in the same sitting. You will only need to set up and get comfortable once.

Talking-head checklist

  1. Phone at eye level. On a tripod, rear camera, lens cleaned.
  2. Face the window. Soft light on your face, nothing bright behind you.
  3. Lapel mic. A hand's width below the chin, tested.
  4. Look at the lens. Sticker beside it as a reminder.
  5. Short chunks. Restart the sentence when you stumble.

With this setup, an hour of recording usually gives enough material for a month of short videos, and the edit can focus on pace and captions rather than rescuing the footage.

Quick answers

How do I look natural talking to the camera?

Set the camera at eye level, look into the lens, speak to one imagined customer, use a bit more energy than usual and record in short chunks. Most people relax after the first few minutes.

Should I memorise a script for talking-head videos?

Usually it is better to speak from a few bullet points in short chunks. Memorised scripts tend to sound stiff unless you are experienced.

What is the best light for filming yourself?

Soft daylight from a window in front of you. Avoid having a window behind you or harsh overhead lights.
