An NGO website donors trust

Donors give to organisations they trust. Before giving, especially for the first time, many will look up the NGO's website and ask: is this real, what do they actually do, where does the money go, and can I get a tax receipt? A website that answers these clearly is a fundraising tool. One that does not quietly loses donations.

Here is what an NGO, charitable trust or non-profit website should include, how to present impact honestly, and how to make donating simple. Tax and foreign contribution rules in India are specific and change over time, so confirm the details with your auditor or legal adviser.

01

A clear mission in one sentence

Visitors should understand within seconds what you do, for whom and where. "We run free evening classes for children of construction workers in Chennai" is clearer than "Empowering communities through holistic development." Specific is credible. Vague sounds like every other organisation asking for money.

Below the mission, introduce the people. Founders and trustees with names, photos and a line about their background, plus the size of the team and how long the organisation has worked. Donors give to people they can identify, and named leadership is one of the simplest signs that an organisation is accountable.

02

Registration and tax details

Show the organisation's legal form (trust, society or section 8 company), registration number and the tax registrations donors care about, such as 12A and 80G status if you hold them. Donors in India often want an 80G receipt, so state clearly whether donations qualify and how receipts are issued.

If you accept foreign contributions, FCRA rules apply, and you must accept them only as permitted. Do not show a foreign donation option unless you are registered and your account setup complies.

Confirm the exact wording of tax and registration details with your auditor before publishing.

03

Show real impact

Real photos of your work, with consent, and short stories of people helped, told with dignity. Numbers that you can stand behind: children taught, meals served, surgeries funded, trees planted, with the year they refer to. Avoid images that strip beneficiaries of dignity, and protect the identity of children and vulnerable people.

04

Be transparent about money

Publish annual reports and audited financial statements, or at least a summary of how funds are used. Explain what a donation buys, for example "₹1,500 covers one child's books for a year", if the figure is accurate. Transparency is the strongest trust signal an NGO has, and it is often missing.

If administration costs are part of your budget, say so honestly and explain what they pay for. Donors increasingly understand that good programmes need staff, rent and audits; what worries them is not knowing.

05

A donation flow that works on phones

Most donors will give on their phone. A clear Donate button on every page, leading to a short form with suggested amounts, UPI and card options and fields needed for the tax receipt, such as name, address and PAN where required. Test the flow on a phone yourself. Send an automatic thank-you and receipt immediately after payment.

06

Volunteering and partnerships

Many visitors want to help in other ways: volunteering, donating goods, corporate partnerships or CSR funding. A page explaining how to get involved, with a simple form, captures them. For CSR partners, a downloadable profile with registration details, past projects and impact figures saves several emails.

Trust is earned in the details

Emotional images may get attention, but donors give when the details reassure them: a real address, named trustees, registration numbers, audited accounts, and evidence that the work happens. Those details turn a moved visitor into a donor.

Show where the money goes, and more of it comes.

Update the site regularly with recent work. A news section last updated two years ago makes donors wonder whether the organisation is still active.

NGO website checklist

  1. Mission. One specific sentence on the home page.
  2. Registration. Legal form, numbers, 12A/80G status if applicable.
  3. Impact. Real photos with consent, honest numbers with dates.
  4. Transparency. Annual reports, financial summaries, trustees.
  5. Donate. Mobile-friendly, with receipt details and instant acknowledgement.

Ask a friend who has never heard of your organisation to visit the site on their phone and try to donate ₹100. Watch where they hesitate. That five-minute test usually reveals the biggest problem.

Quick answers

What should an NGO website include?

A clear mission, registration and tax details, real evidence of impact, financial transparency such as annual reports, a simple mobile donation flow, and ways to volunteer or partner.

Should an NGO show its 80G status on its website?

If the organisation holds 80G registration, stating it clearly helps donors who want tax receipts. Confirm the exact details with your auditor before publishing.

Can an Indian NGO accept foreign donations through its website?

Only if it is registered under FCRA, or has prior permission, and receives the funds into the designated account as the rules require. Check current FCRA rules with a legal adviser.
