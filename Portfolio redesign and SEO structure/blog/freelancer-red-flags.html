Red flags to spot before you pay an advance

Most stories about freelance designers going wrong follow the same pattern. The logo turned out to be a template. The files never arrived. The designer stopped replying after the advance. Looking back, the warning signs were usually there from the first conversation, but nobody knew what to look for.

As a freelancer myself, I want clients to hire carefully. Good freelancers benefit when clients know how to spot bad ones. Here are the red flags I would watch for, whether you hire locally, on Upwork, Fiverr or through Instagram.

01

A portfolio you cannot verify

A portfolio full of polished mockups with no names, no context and no explanation is worth questioning. Some designers show work they did not do, or concepts that were never used by a real business.

Ask about two or three projects: who the client was, what the brief was, what problem the design solved. A genuine designer can talk about their work easily. A reverse image search of a logo or two takes a minute and sometimes reveals the same design on stock sites.

02

Prices that are far too low

A logo for ₹500 with "unlimited revisions" and delivery in 24 hours is possible only if very little design is happening. Extremely low prices often mean templates, clip art or copied work, which can cause legal trouble later if you try to trademark the logo.

This does not mean expensive is always better. It means the price should make sense for the work described.

03

No written scope or quote

If the designer will not put the deliverables, timeline, number of revisions and payment terms in writing, even in a simple message, walk away. Most disputes come from different assumptions about what was included.

A good quote says what you get, by when, how many revision rounds, what files will be delivered and when payments are due.

04

Vague answers about files and ownership

Ask directly: "Will I get the vector files? Will I own the design?" A designer who hesitates, says you only get a JPG, or charges a large extra fee just for the source files after the fact, is setting up a dependency.

You should receive the files you need to use the design anywhere, and written confirmation that it is yours once paid.

A designer can reasonably charge for extra file formats or the editable working file, as long as this is stated in the quote before you agree.

05

Pressure to pay everything upfront

An advance is normal. Many freelancers, including me, ask for 50% to book a project. But being pushed to pay 100% before any work, especially to a new contact with no track record, is a risk. Staged payments protect both sides.

06

Poor communication from the start

If replies are slow, vague or inconsistent before you have paid, they rarely improve after. Notice whether the designer asks questions about your business. A designer who jumps straight to price and delivery without understanding what you need is likely to produce generic work.

07

Promises that sound too good

"Unlimited revisions", "guaranteed to go viral" and "ready in two hours" are marketing lines, not plans. Unlimited revisions usually means unlimited small tweaks of one weak idea. A professional sets clear limits because they plan to get it right.

Good freelancers welcome questions

Asking about past work, files, ownership and payment terms is not rude. Professional freelancers expect these questions and answer them easily, because clear terms protect them too. If someone becomes defensive when you ask, that tells you something important.

The best time to discover a problem is before the advance, not after it.

Platform reviews help, but read them carefully. Look for reviews that mention communication, deadlines and files, not just five stars and "great work".

Questions to ask before hiring

  1. Portfolio. Can you tell me about this project and the client?
  2. Process. What happens between the brief and the first concepts?
  3. Scope. What exactly is included, and how many revision rounds?
  4. Files. Which file formats will I receive at the end?
  5. Ownership. Will the design be fully mine once paid?
  6. Payment. What advance do you need, and when is the balance due?

Clear, confident answers to these six questions are a good sign. Hesitation or changing answers are worth paying attention to.

Quick answers

How do I know if a freelance designer is genuine?

Ask them to explain past projects, check that their portfolio work is original with a quick reverse image search, and make sure they provide a clear written scope and quote.

Is it safe to pay a freelance designer in advance?

A partial advance, often around 50%, is normal and reasonable. Be cautious about paying the full amount upfront to someone with no track record.

Why are some logo designs so cheap online?

Very cheap logos often rely on templates, clip art or reused designs. They can cause problems if you later want to trademark the logo or need original files.
