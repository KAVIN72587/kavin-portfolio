Colour correction or colour grading?

Two clips from the same event, shot ten minutes apart, can look like they came from different days. One is orange, one is blue, one is too dark. Fixing that is colour correction. Making the whole film feel a certain way is grading.

Editors use both terms, and quotes often bundle them as "colour". Knowing the difference helps you understand what you are paying for, and why a cheap edit can look noticeably flat or patchy next to a properly finished one.

01

Correction fixes what the camera got wrong

Colour correction is technical. It balances exposure so faces are not too dark, removes colour casts from tube lights or sunsets, and matches clips so that skin tones stay the same from shot to shot. The goal is footage that looks natural and consistent, not a particular style.

It matters most when footage comes from several sources: two cameras, a drone and someone's phone, or a shoot that moved from indoors to outdoors. Every camera records colour slightly differently.

02

Grading sets the mood

Grading comes after correction. It is the creative look: warm and soft for a wedding, clean and neutral for a corporate film, punchy and high-contrast for a product reel. A good grade is consistent across the whole video and supports what the video is trying to say.

Grading cannot rescue badly corrected footage. If the clips do not match first, a stylish look only makes the differences more obvious.

03

Skin tones are the test

Viewers forgive a lot, but they notice when people look grey, orange or sunburnt. Indian skin tones in particular suffer under careless correction and harsh filters. A good editor protects skin first and builds the look around it.

Watch the draft on your phone at normal brightness. If faces look wrong there, they look wrong to most of your audience.

04

Log footage needs grading by default

Many cameras, and some phones, can record in a flat "log" profile that keeps more detail in highlights and shadows. It looks washed out until it is graded. If your videographer shot in log, colour work is not optional; it is part of making the footage usable at all.

05

Filters and LUTs are a starting point

A LUT is a preset look applied in one click. Used well, it is a quick starting point. Used alone, it applies the same adjustment to every shot regardless of lighting, which is why template edits often look patchy. Proper colour work adjusts shots individually after the preset.

06

Brand colours need care

For product and corporate videos, colour accuracy is not only about mood. If your packaging is a particular red, customers expect to see that red, not an orange or a pink caused by a warm grade. The same goes for uniforms, signage and logos that appear in the footage.

A good editor checks these against a reference, such as the product itself photographed in neutral light or your brand colour codes, and adjusts the grade so the product stays true even when the rest of the frame is stylised. If the colour of your product matters to buyers, mention it in the brief so it is treated as a priority rather than an afterthought.

Ask what "colour" means in the quote

Some quotes include a quick preset over everything. Others include shot-by-shot correction and a consistent grade. Both may say "colour graded". The difference shows in the final video, especially on weddings and corporate films with mixed lighting.

Correct first so it matches, then grade so it feels right.

Ask whether clips are matched individually and whether skin tones are checked. If you have a reference look you love, send a link: it is far easier to aim at a picture than at a word like "cinematic".

Help your editor get the colour right

  1. Tell them the cameras. Which camera or phone shot what, and whether anything was shot in log.
  2. Send a reference. One or two videos or stills with the look you want.
  3. Say where it will play. Phone, laptop, projector or LED wall all show colour differently.
  4. Flag brand colours. Logos and products should look like the real thing, not tinted by the grade.

Five minutes spent on these notes saves at least one revision round, because the first draft is already aimed at the look you had in mind.

Quick answers

What is the difference between colour correction and colour grading?

Colour correction fixes technical problems such as exposure, white balance and mismatched clips so footage looks natural and consistent. Colour grading then applies a creative look across the whole video.

Is colour grading included in video editing?

It depends on the editor. Many include basic correction and a simple look; detailed shot-by-shot work may be included or charged separately. Ask exactly what the quote covers.

Can colour grading fix badly shot footage?

It can improve exposure and colour within limits, but it cannot recover blown-out highlights, heavy noise or out-of-focus shots. Good lighting at the shoot still matters most.
