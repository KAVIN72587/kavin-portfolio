The event recap video, delivered fast

A launch, a conference, a store opening, an annual day. For about forty-eight hours afterwards, the people who were there want to relive it and share it. That is the window for a recap video. Miss it and the same video gets a fraction of the attention.

Here is how to plan an event recap so it is ready quickly: what to shoot, how long it should be, what the editor needs from you, and the small decisions that save days.

01

Decide the purpose first

A recap for attendees to share is different from a recap meant to sell next year's tickets or to show sponsors what they got. The first is emotional: faces, energy, moments. The second shows scale, crowd, speakers and branding. A sponsor recap needs their logos clearly visible. Tell the videographer and editor which it is before the event, not after.

02

60 to 90 seconds is enough

A recap is not a record of the event. It is a highlight. Sixty to ninety seconds covers the arrival, the key moments, the people and the atmosphere. A vertical version of 30 seconds suits reels and stories. If you need the full talks or ceremony, that is a separate, longer edit and can follow later without delaying the recap.

03

Shots the editor will need

Wide shots that show scale. Close-ups of faces reacting, laughing, clapping. The venue setup and branding. Key moments: the ribbon cutting, the speaker's best line, the award. Movement: people arriving, crowd walking, hands shaking. And a few seconds of clean audio from the strongest speech moment, which often becomes the backbone of the recap.

Shoot some clips vertically as well, or wide enough to crop, so the reel version does not lose heads and logos.

04

Plan for speed

Fast delivery is planned, not rushed. Book the editor in advance for the day after the event. Agree the music beforehand. Send the logo, any speaker names and spelling for on-screen titles, and the sponsor list before the event. Ask the videographer to mark the key moments with timestamps, or flag them in a message as they happen.

Uploading footage the same night, rather than days later, is often the single biggest time saver.

05

One reviewer, one round

Recaps lose their timing in approval loops. Agree in advance that one person will review the first draft and send consolidated feedback the same day. A quick, decisive round gets the video out while people are still talking about the event.

06

Use it more than once

The recap is useful long after the event: on the website's events page, in sponsor proposals for next year, in a LinkedIn post about the company's culture, and as an opening at the next edition. Ask for a version without dated text, such as "See you next year", so it ages well.

Keep the raw footage too, organised by moment. Next year, clips from this event can open the promotional video for the next one, and a library of real event footage is far more convincing to future sponsors and attendees than any description of the atmosphere.

Timing is the quality that matters most

A recap edited beautifully over three weeks usually performs worse than a good one delivered the next day. Attendees tag each other, share it in groups and comment while the memory is fresh. That reach is free, and it fades quickly.

A good recap tomorrow beats a perfect one next month.

If you need a polished long-form film too, do both: a fast recap for sharing, then the detailed edit afterwards.

Send the editor before the event

  1. Purpose. Attendee share, sponsor proof or next year's promotion.
  2. Logos and sponsor list. In high resolution.
  3. Names for titles. Speakers and guests, spelled correctly.
  4. Music direction. A reference track or mood.
  5. Reviewer. One person, available the day after.

With these in hand before the event, the editor can set up the project, titles and music the day before, and start cutting the moment footage arrives. That is how a recap goes out in a day or two without being rushed.

After the event, the only thing left is getting the footage across quickly. A shared Drive folder created in advance, with the videographer's upload starting the same night, removes the most common delay of all.

Quick answers

How long should an event recap video be?

Around 60 to 90 seconds for the main recap, plus a 30-second vertical version for reels and stories. Full talks or ceremonies are a separate edit.

How fast can an event recap video be delivered?

With footage uploaded the same night and approvals planned, a recap can often be delivered within a few days of the event. Planning ahead matters more than rushing the edit.

What should I shoot for an event recap?

Wide shots for scale, close-ups of faces and reactions, venue branding, key moments, people arriving, and a few seconds of the strongest speech with clean audio.
